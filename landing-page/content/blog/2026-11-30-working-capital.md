Plenty of shops make a profit on paper and still cannot pay the distributor on Monday. The money has not vanished. It is sitting on the shelves as stock, in the udhaar khata as unpaid bills, and in the GST you collected but have not yet paid. That money is your **working capital**, and how fast it turns back into cash matters as much as your margin.

Most pages on working capital are written for company balance sheets. This one is for a kirana, a medical store or a small service business: the formula, the working capital cycle with real shop numbers, how to free up cash without borrowing, and how cash credit and overdraft limits work.

## Quick answer

- **Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities.** For a shop: (cash + bank + stock at cost + udhaar to collect) − (supplier dues + GST payable + overdraft used + bills due).
- **Working capital cycle = Inventory days + Receivable days − Payable days.** It is the number of days your money is stuck between paying for stock and getting paid for it.
- **Shorter is better.** The three levers: hold less slow stock, collect udhaar faster, get longer (honest) supplier credit.
- **Growth needs cash.** If sales rise 25%, the money tied up in stock and udhaar rises about 25% too, even if profit is fine.
- **Borrowing:** banks give working capital as a **cash credit (CC)** or **overdraft (OD)** limit, where you pay interest only on what you use. Mudra loans can also be working capital limits.
- **The MSME 45-day rule** helps micro and small **manufacturers and service providers** get paid by business buyers; it does not cover retail or wholesale traders as suppliers.

## The working capital formula

**Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities**

"Current" means anything that turns into cash, or has to be paid, within about a year. For a shop, that looks like this:

| Current assets | Current liabilities |
| --- | --- |
| Cash in the drawer | Amounts owed to suppliers and distributors |
| Bank and UPI balance | GST collected but not yet paid |
| Stock, valued at cost | Overdraft or cash credit used |
| Udhaar (money customers owe you) | Rent, salaries and electricity due |
| Advances paid to suppliers | Short-term loans repayable within a year |

### Worked example: Sharma General Store

| Current assets | ₹ | Current liabilities | ₹ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash in drawer | 25,000 | Supplier dues | 1,70,000 |
| Bank and UPI balance | 60,000 | GST payable this month | 10,000 |
| Stock at cost | 4,25,000 | Overdraft used | 1,00,000 |
| Udhaar outstanding | 1,20,000 | Rent and salary due | 20,000 |
| **Total** | **6,30,000** | **Total** | **3,00,000** |

**Working capital = ₹6,30,000 − ₹3,00,000 = ₹3,30,000.**

Two ratios tell you more than the rupee figure:

- **Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities = 6,30,000 ÷ 3,00,000 = 2.1.** Above 1 means short-term assets cover short-term dues.
- **Quick ratio = (Current assets − Stock) ÷ Current liabilities = 2,05,000 ÷ 3,00,000 = 0.68.** It leaves out stock, because you cannot pay a supplier with packets of dal.

If every supplier asked for payment this week, Sharma could not pay without selling stock first. That is normal for a shop, and it is why the **cycle** matters more than the snapshot.

## The working capital cycle

The cycle measures **how many days a rupee spends away from you**, from paying for stock to collecting from the customer.

**Working capital cycle = Inventory days + Receivable days − Payable days**

| Part | Formula | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory days | Stock at cost ÷ Daily cost of goods sold | Days stock sits before it sells |
| Receivable days | Udhaar outstanding ÷ Daily sales | Days, on average, before sales turn into cash |
| Payable days | Supplier dues ÷ Daily cost of goods sold | Days of credit your suppliers give you |

For a full year, use average balances and 365 days (average stock ÷ yearly cost of goods sold × 365, and so on). A monthly check with daily figures works fine for a shop.

### Worked example: the cycle for Sharma General Store

Sharma's store sells **₹20,000 a day** at a **15% gross margin**, so its **cost of goods sold is ₹17,000 a day**. About 20% of sales are on udhaar.

| Part | Working | Days |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory days | ₹4,25,000 ÷ ₹17,000 | **25** |
| Receivable days | ₹1,20,000 ÷ ₹20,000 | **6** |
| Payable days | ₹1,70,000 ÷ ₹17,000 | **10** |
| **Working capital cycle** | 25 + 6 − 10 | **21 days** |

The money stuck in the cycle is **stock + udhaar − supplier dues = ₹4,25,000 + ₹1,20,000 − ₹1,70,000 = ₹3,75,000.** Sharma needs that much of his own money or borrowed money just to keep the shop running at this level of sales.

Receivable days of 6 is averaged over all sales, including cash and UPI. The credit customers themselves take about 30 days (₹1,20,000 ÷ ₹4,000 of credit sales a day).

### What happens when Sharma tightens the cycle

| Change | New figure | Cash freed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cut slow stock: inventory 25 → 20 days | Stock ₹3,40,000 | ₹85,000 |
| Collect udhaar faster: 6 → 3 days | Udhaar ₹60,000 | ₹60,000 |
| Agree longer supplier credit: 10 → 14 days | Supplier dues ₹2,38,000 | ₹68,000 |
| **Total** | **Cycle 20 + 3 − 14 = 9 days** | **₹2,13,000** |

Money tied up falls from ₹3,75,000 to **₹1,62,000** (₹3,40,000 + ₹60,000 − ₹2,38,000). If that ₹2,13,000 had been sitting on an overdraft at, say, 12% a year, the saving is ₹2,13,000 × 12% = **₹25,560 a year**, about ₹2,130 a month, without selling a single extra packet.

## How udhaar and stock tie up cash

**Udhaar** is an interest-free loan to your customers: in the example, six full days of sales are permanently out. At a 15% margin, one unpaid ₹1,000 wipes out the profit on about ₹6,700 of sales. Our guide to [udhaar management](https://billzap.app/blog/udhaar-management) has credit limits, due dates and reminder messages that cut collection time without losing customers.

**Stock** is cash on a shelf. Milk and atta turn in days; some cosmetics and gift items hold cash for months; expired stock is cash lost for good. Our guide to [inventory management techniques](https://billzap.app/blog/inventory-management-techniques) covers reorder levels, ABC analysis and inventory days item by item, which is where most of the ₹85,000 in the example comes from.

**Growth eats cash.** If Sharma's sales rise 25% and the cycle stays at 21 days, the money tied up rises about 25%: ₹3,75,000 × 25% = **₹93,750 more**. That is why growing shops often feel poorer. Plan for it before adding a new range or counter.

## GST and your working capital

GST affects cash timing even though it is not your money.

- **GST collected sits with you for a while.** A monthly filer pays the GST for a month with GSTR-3B by the 20th of the next month. That float helps, but treat it as a liability: spend it on stock and you will be short on the 20th. See our [GST due dates](https://billzap.app/blog/gst-due-dates) for the full calendar, including quarterly (QRMP) and composition dates.
- **Unused input tax credit** is cash you have already paid, locked in the GST ledger.
- **Pay suppliers within 180 days.** If a registered buyer does not pay a supplier the value plus GST within 180 days of the invoice date, the input tax credit claimed on it has to be reversed with interest, and can be reclaimed when payment is made. Stretching suppliers too far costs you credit.

## Ways to improve working capital

**Stock**

1. **Find slow movers monthly** and stop reordering them; clear dead stock with combos.
2. **Order smaller and more often** on items your distributor delivers weekly.
3. **Check schemes first.** "Buy 10 cartons, get 1 free" is worth little if the extra stock sits for two months.

**Udhaar**

4. **Set a credit limit and due date** for every udhaar customer.
5. **Send reminders on a schedule**, with a UPI link so paying takes one tap.
6. **Push UPI at the counter** so more sales settle instantly.

**Suppliers**

7. **Ask for a few more days of credit**; paying on the agreed date every time is what earns it.
8. **Time big purchases** so the due date falls after your busy days.
9. **Use early-payment discounts only when they beat your borrowing cost.** A 1% discount for paying 30 days early is worth about 12% a year, roughly what a bank overdraft costs, so it is not a free gain if you borrow to take it.

**Cash habits**

10. **Close the drawer daily** so you know your real cash position each evening.
11. **Keep personal and shop money separate.** Household withdrawals from the drawer quietly drain working capital.
12. **Review the cycle monthly.**

## The MSME 45-day rule as a supplier lever

If **you sell to other businesses**, the law can help you get paid. Under **Sections 15 and 16 of the MSMED Act, 2006**, a buyer must pay a micro or small enterprise supplier within the agreed period, which cannot exceed **45 days** from acceptance of the goods or services (**15 days** if there is no written agreement). Late payment attracts **compound interest with monthly rests at three times the bank rate** notified by RBI.

The income-tax side adds pressure. Under **Section 37(2)(g) of the Income-tax Act, 2025** (formerly Section 43B(h) of the 1961 Act, which still governs years up to FY 2025-26), a buyer can deduct an amount owed to a micro or small enterprise beyond that time limit only in the year it is actually paid. Large buyers (companies with turnover above ₹250 crore, and central public sector enterprises) must also be onboarded on TReDS, where MSME suppliers can discount accepted invoices for early cash.

**Who can use it:** registered micro and small **manufacturers and service providers**, such as a bakery supplying cafes, a printing unit, a fabrication workshop or a catering service. **Retail and wholesale traders** can register on Udyam, but their MSME benefits are restricted to priority sector lending, so a kirana selling to an office canteen cannot rely on the 45-day rule. Full details in our [MSME 45 days payment rule](https://billzap.app/blog/msme-45-days-payment-rule) guide.

## Working capital loans: CC, OD and others

When your own money is not enough, banks lend for working capital mainly through **limits**, not lump-sum loans.

| Option | How it works | Typical cost (illustrative, check your lender) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cash credit (CC)** | Limit against your stock and receivables; you draw up to the "drawing power" | Bank's benchmark rate plus a spread; interest only on the amount used |
| **Overdraft (OD)** | Limit on your current account, sometimes against a fixed deposit or property | Similar to CC; OD against an FD is usually cheapest |
| **Mudra working capital limit** | Collateral-free CC or OD under PMMY, often with a Mudra RuPay card | Bank or lender's own rate; no fixed Mudra rate |
| **Supplier credit** | Pay the distributor in 7 to 30 days | Free if paid on time |
| **Invoice discounting (TReDS)** | For MSME suppliers to large buyers | Discount rate set by bidding financiers |
| **NBFC or app business loan** | Lump sum repaid in EMIs | Often about 12% to 28% a year or more, plus fees |

Things to know before you apply:

- **Interest only on what you use.** On a ₹3 lakh CC limit where you use ₹1.5 lakh on average at 11% a year, interest is ₹1,50,000 × 11% = **₹16,500 a year**, about ₹1,375 a month, not ₹33,000.
- **Drawing power** on a CC is usually your stock plus eligible receivables, minus a margin the bank keeps (commonly around 25%), so you may not be able to use the full limit. Banks ask for a monthly or quarterly stock statement.
- **How big a limit?** RBI's guidance, based on the Nayak Committee, is that working capital limits for small units are assessed at a **minimum of 20% of projected annual turnover**, for limits up to ₹5 crore. A shop with ₹72 lakh of projected sales could expect the assessment to start from about ₹14.4 lakh, subject to the bank's own appraisal.
- **Collateral:** banks must not take collateral on loans up to **₹20 lakh** to micro and small enterprises sanctioned or renewed from 1 April 2026. Our guides to the [Mudra loan](https://billzap.app/blog/mudra-loan) and [business loans without collateral](https://billzap.app/blog/business-loan-without-collateral) cover the schemes.
- **Rates are benchmark-linked.** Most bank MSME loans are an external benchmark rate (linked to RBI's repo rate) plus a spread, reset periodically. Ask for the benchmark, spread and reset date. Limits are reviewed yearly.

A working capital loan smooths the cycle; it does not fix a cycle that keeps getting longer.

## FAQ

### What is working capital in simple words?

The money a business needs to run day to day: what turns into cash soon (cash, bank, stock, udhaar) minus what you must pay soon (suppliers, GST, overdraft, bills).

### What is the working capital formula?

Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities. The working capital cycle is Inventory days + Receivable days − Payable days.

### What is a good working capital ratio for a small business?

A current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities) between about 1.2 and 2 is usually comfortable. Below 1 means you may struggle to pay short-term dues. Very high can mean too much cash stuck in stock or udhaar.

### How can a kirana store reduce its working capital cycle?

Stop reordering slow items, order fast movers in smaller lots, set udhaar limits and due dates with regular reminders, push UPI payments, and negotiate a few more days of supplier credit by always paying on the agreed date.

### What is the difference between cash credit and overdraft?

Cash credit is a limit secured on your stock and receivables, with drawing power based on stock statements. An overdraft is a limit on your current account, often secured by a fixed deposit or property. In both, interest is charged only on the amount used.

### Does the MSME 45-day rule apply to kirana stores?

Not as a supplier. Retail and wholesale traders registered on Udyam get MSME benefits only for priority sector lending, so the 45-day payment protection is for micro and small manufacturers and service providers.

## The bottom line

Profit tells you whether the business is worth running; working capital tells you whether it can keep running. **Work out your cycle monthly: inventory days plus udhaar days minus supplier days.** Every day you cut frees about a day of cost of sales in cash. Start with slow stock and old udhaar, and use a CC or OD limit to smooth the busy season, not to cover a growing cycle.
