Search "stock register format" and you mostly get downloadable templates: Excel sheets, Scribd uploads, Pinterest pins of ruled ledger paper and vendors selling inventory software. A template gives you empty columns. It does not tell a shop owner how to fill them, how often, what to do when the count on paper and the count on the shelf disagree, or what GST actually expects you to keep.

This guide does. It sets out the columns, gives a filled kirana sample you can copy, and covers the habits that keep it accurate: regular counts, reorder levels, recording damage and expiry, and valuing stock at cost.

## Quick answer

- A **stock register** (also called a stock book or inventory register) records, item by item, how much stock you **started with, received, sold or lost, and have left**.
- The core columns are: **date, item and unit, opening stock, received, sold, other outward (damaged, expired, lost), closing stock, cost rate and value**.
- The one formula: **Closing stock = Opening + Received − Sold − Other outward.** Value = Closing × cost price.
- **Update fast-moving items daily**, close the register weekly or monthly, and **count the shelf** at least once a month for your top items.
- For GST-registered shops, **Section 35** of the CGST Act requires records of goods in and out and of stock, and **Rule 56(2)** spells out what the stock account must show (composition dealers are excused from Rule 56(2)). Keep the records for **72 months** from the due date of that year's annual return.
- **Value stock at cost, not MRP.**

## What a stock register is, and what it is not

A stock register answers one question for every item: **how many do I have, and how did it get to that number?** It is about quantities first and rupees second.

It is different from the registers it sits beside:

| Register | What it records | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purchase register** | Supplier bills: who, when, how much, GST paid | Rupees |
| **Sales register** | Your bills: to whom, how much, GST charged | Rupees |
| **Stock register** | Movement of each item: in, out, balance | Quantity (then rupees) |

Purchases feed the "received" column and sales feed the "sold" column. If you bill every sale, half the work is done; see our guide to [bill formats](https://billzap.app/blog/bill-format).

## The columns of a stock register

| Column | What to write | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Date** or period | The day, or the week/month for a summary | Ties every figure to a time |
| **Item name and unit** | "Toor dal 1 kg pkt", "Sugar (loose), kg" | Mixing units is the commonest error |
| **Opening stock** | Last period's closing stock | Never start from memory |
| **Received (inward)** | Quantity from supplier, with bill number | Links to the purchase bill |
| **Sold (outward)** | Quantity billed to customers | Links to your bills |
| **Other outward** | Damaged, expired, lost, free samples, own use, with a reason | This is where shrinkage shows up |
| **Closing stock** | Opening + Received − Sold − Other outward | The number you should find on the shelf |
| **Cost rate** | Purchase price per unit (before GST if you claim credit) | Used to value stock |
| **Value** | Closing stock × cost rate | Your money sitting on the shelf |
| **Reorder level** and **status** | The quantity at which you reorder | Turns the register into a buying list |

Small shops usually keep the register in one of two shapes: an **item ledger** (one page per item, every movement on its own line, with a running balance), or a **summary sheet** (one line per item per week or month). Most kiranas do well with a summary sheet for everything plus item ledgers for the 10 to 20 items that sell fastest or cost most.

## A filled sample for a kirana store

Here is a weekly summary for six items, for the week of 19 to 25 October 2026. Copy it into a notebook or a spreadsheet and replace the items with yours.

| Item (unit) | Opening | Received | Sold | Other out | Closing | Cost rate (₹) | Value (₹) | Reorder level | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Toor dal (1 kg pkt) | 40 | 50 | 62 | 0 | 28 | 128 | 3,584 | 30 | Reorder |
| Sugar, loose (kg) | 75 | 100 | 118 | 2 (spilt) | 55 | 42 | 2,310 | 40 | OK |
| Sunflower oil (1 L pouch) | 30 | 24 | 35 | 1 (leaked) | 18 | 145 | 2,610 | 20 | Reorder |
| Glucose biscuits (250 g pkt) | 60 | 48 | 70 | 0 | 38 | 22 | 836 | 24 | OK |
| Bath soap (100 g) | 48 | 0 | 21 | 0 | 27 | 32 | 864 | 12 | OK |
| Wheat atta (5 kg bag) | 20 | 15 | 22 | 0 | 13 | 225 | 2,925 | 10 | OK |
| **Total stock value** | | | | | | | **13,129** | | |

Check any line with the formula. Toor dal: 40 + 50 − 62 − 0 = **28**, and 28 × ₹128 = **₹3,584**. Sunflower oil: 30 + 24 − 35 − 1 = **18**, and 18 × ₹145 = **₹2,610**.

And here is the item ledger for sunflower oil for the same week, which is what produces that summary line:

| Date | Details | In | Out | Balance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 19 Oct | Opening stock | | | 30 |
| 19 Oct | Sales | | 7 | 23 |
| 20 Oct | Received, supplier bill no. 2291 | 24 | | 47 |
| 20 Oct | Sales | | 6 | 41 |
| 21 Oct | Sales | | 5 | 36 |
| 22 Oct | One pouch leaked, written off | | 1 | 35 |
| 22 Oct | Sales | | 4 | 31 |
| 24 Oct | Sales | | 8 | 23 |
| 25 Oct | Sales | | 5 | 18 |

Sales add up to 7 + 6 + 5 + 4 + 8 + 5 = 35, the leaked pouch is the 1 in "other out", and the balance ends at 18, matching the summary.

### Doing it in Excel

If you prefer a spreadsheet, put the columns in this order: A Item, B Opening, C Received, D Sold, E Other out, F Closing, G Cost rate, H Value, I Reorder level, J Status. Then in row 2:

- **F2:** `=B2+C2-D2-E2`
- **H2:** `=F2*G2`
- **J2:** `=IF(F2<=I2,"Reorder","OK")`

Copy the row down. Each new week, copy column F into column B of the new sheet. Carrying closing stock forward as the next opening stock is the step most free templates never explain.

## How often to update it

- **Daily, for fast movers.** Staples like dal, sugar, oil, atta and milk products move every day. Note receipts as goods arrive and total the day's sales each evening.
- **Weekly or monthly, for slow movers.** Soap, stationery and spices can be summarised once a week or month.
- **Every delivery, without exception.** Write the supplier's bill number next to the quantity received. When a supplier's bill and your register disagree, that number settles it.
- **Close the register** at the end of every month, and carry each closing figure forward as the next opening figure.

## The physical stock check

A register that is never compared with the shelf slowly drifts away from reality. Count the actual stock and compare:

- **Top 20 items:** count monthly, or weekly if they are costly or easy to pilfer.
- **Everything:** count once a year, ideally on **31 March**, because that closing stock goes into your year-end accounts.

Count before opening or after closing, not while selling. When the count differs from the register, **do not just overwrite the closing figure**. Record the difference in "other outward" with a reason:

| Item | Register says | Counted | Difference | Likely reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Toor dal (1 kg pkt) | 28 | 27 | −1 | Torn packet thrown away, not recorded |
| Sugar, loose (kg) | 55 | 53.5 | −1.5 | Weighing losses on loose sales |

Small, steady losses on loose items are normal. A growing difference on the same packaged item points to unrecorded sales, a billing mistake or theft.

## Reorder levels: turn the register into a buying list

A reorder level is the quantity at which you place the next order, so that stock arrives before the shelf is empty:

**Reorder level = average daily sales × days the supplier takes to deliver + safety stock**

From the sample: toor dal sold 62 packets in 7 days, about **9 a day**. The distributor delivers in **2 days**, and you want **12 packets** extra for a rush. Reorder level = 9 × 2 + 12 = **30**. Closing stock is 28, so it is time to order.

Sunflower oil sold 35 in 7 days, **5 a day**, with a **3-day** delivery and **5** spare: 5 × 3 + 5 = **20**. Closing stock is 18, so order oil too.

Raise reorder levels before festivals. Running out of fast movers loses more sales than almost anything else; our guide on how to [increase kirana store sales](https://billzap.app/blog/increase-kirana-store-sales) has more on this.

## What GST law asks you to keep

If your shop is **GST-registered**, a stock register is not optional.

- **Section 35(1)** of the CGST Act requires every registered person to keep a true and correct account of, among other things, **inward and outward supply of goods** and **stock of goods**, at the principal place of business.
- **Rule 56(2)** says the stock account must show the **opening balance, receipt, supply, goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or disposed of by way of gift or free sample, and the balance of stock**. That is exactly the column set above. Composition dealers (Section 10) are not covered by Rule 56(2), though keeping a simple register is still sensible. See our guide to the [GST composition scheme](https://billzap.app/blog/gst-composition-scheme).
- **Section 35(6):** if goods are not accounted for, the officer can work out tax on them **as if they had been sold**. A register that does not match the shelf invites exactly that.
- **Section 36:** keep the accounts for **72 months from the due date of the annual return** for that year. For FY 2026-27, whose annual return is due on 31 December 2027, that means keeping the records until 31 December 2033.
- **Rule 56(16):** a register kept on paper must be kept at the place of business shown in your registration; one kept digitally must be accessible there.

There is also a tax cost to shrinkage. Under **Section 17(5)(h)**, input tax credit is not available on goods **lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or given away as gifts or free samples**. If you claimed credit when you bought a carton that later expired and was thrown out, that credit generally has to be reversed. Your "other outward" column is the record your accountant needs to work this out.

## Valuing stock: cost, not MRP

Value closing stock at **what you paid**, not what you will sell it for. Valuing at MRP counts profit you have not earned yet. In the sample, 18 pouches of oil at the ₹145 cost price are worth ₹2,610; at an MRP of ₹165 they would show ₹2,970, overstating stock by ₹360 on one item alone.

Two refinements:

- **When the purchase price changes**, use either the latest price or a weighted average, and stick to one method. Weighted average for toor dal, if the 40 opening packets cost ₹124 and the 50 received cost ₹128: (40 × 124 + 50 × 128) ÷ 90 = ₹11,360 ÷ 90 = **₹126.22** a packet. The sample uses the latest price for simplicity.
- **Damaged or near-expiry stock** should be valued at cost or at what you can realistically sell it for, whichever is lower. A ₹145 pouch you will clear at ₹100 is worth ₹100 in your stock figure.

Closing stock feeds directly into profit: **cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − closing stock**. Overstate closing stock and your profit looks bigger than it is. Our [profit margin formula](https://billzap.app/blog/profit-margin-formula) guide shows the rest of that calculation.

## Common mistakes

- **Not recording shrinkage.** Spillage, torn packets, rats, a few biscuits for the children: each one quietly makes the register wrong. Give it a line.
- **Ignoring expiry.** Note expiry dates for packaged food, medicines and dairy when goods arrive, and sell the oldest stock first. Expired stock goes in "other outward" when it leaves the shelf, not months later.
- **Mixing units.** Sugar bought in 50 kg bags and sold by the kilo must be recorded in kilos throughout. Cartons and pieces need a fixed conversion (1 carton = 48 soaps).
- **Filling sales from memory.** Write sales from your bills, not from a guess at the end of the week.
- **Overwriting after a count.** Record the difference with a reason so you can see a pattern.
- **Forgetting returns.** Goods sent back to a supplier are outward; goods a customer returns come back in.

## FAQ

### What is the format of a stock register?

Date, item and unit, opening stock, received, sold, other outward (damaged, expired, lost), closing stock, cost rate and value. Closing stock = opening + received − sold − other outward.

### Is a stock register compulsory under GST?

For regular GST-registered businesses, yes. Section 35 requires records of goods in, goods out and stock, and Rule 56(2) lists what the stock account must show. Composition dealers are excused from Rule 56(2). Records must be kept for 72 months from the due date of that year's annual return.

### How often should a small shop update its stock register?

Daily for fast-moving items, weekly or monthly for slow ones, and at every delivery. Count the shelf monthly for your top items and fully once a year, ideally on 31 March.

### Can I keep a stock register in Excel?

Yes. Use one row per item with the formula `=Opening+Received-Sold-Other` for closing stock and `=Closing*Cost` for value, and carry each closing figure forward as the next opening figure. A digital register is accepted under GST if it is accessible at your place of business.

### What should I do with expired or damaged stock?

Record it in the "other outward" column with the date and reason, value it at the lower of cost and what it can fetch, and tell your accountant, because input tax credit claimed on goods written off generally has to be reversed.

## The bottom line

A stock register answers one question: how many do I have, and why? Carry closing stock forward, record every delivery with its bill number, give damage and expiry their own line, count the shelf regularly and value at cost. Done that way, it is your buying list, your theft alarm and, if you are GST-registered, the record the law already asks for.
