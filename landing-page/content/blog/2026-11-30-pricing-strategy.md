Most pricing guides are written for startups: subscription tiers, freemium, "penetration pricing". A shop owner in India asks different questions. What do I charge for a packet with an MRP already printed on it? Do I quote my service with GST or without? Should I match the shop across the road? And why does a 10% discount feel small to the customer and big at the end of the month?

This guide answers them with Indian numbers: the five pricing strategies that matter, the MRP rules you cannot break, GST maths for inclusive prices, and worked examples for a kirana item and a repair service.

## Quick answer

- **Start with your full cost** (purchase price plus freight, wastage and, for a service, your time and overheads). That is your floor.
- **Cost-plus pricing**: for a **target margin**, use **Price = Cost ÷ (1 − margin)**. A 25% markup gives only a 20% margin.
- **Competitor-based pricing** checks what nearby shops and apps charge; **value-based pricing** asks what it is worth to the customer. Cost is the floor, value the ceiling.
- **Psychological (₹99) and bundle pricing** are finishing touches, not strategies on their own.
- **MRP is a ceiling, and it includes all taxes.** Under the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011, no one may sell a packaged item above its MRP, and you cannot add GST on top of it. Selling below MRP is allowed.
- **GST-registered regular shops price before GST**: taxable value = inclusive price × 100 ÷ (100 + rate). The GST you collect is not income.
- **Discounts cost more than they look.** On a 20% margin, a 10% discount means selling **twice** as many units to earn the same profit.

## The five pricing strategies at a glance

| Strategy | How it works | Best for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost-plus** | Cost + a fixed markup | Loose goods, own-packed items, services | Ignores what customers will pay |
| **Competitor-based** | Price near what others charge | Branded FMCG and staples | A price war you cannot win |
| **Value-based** | Price on what it is worth to the buyer | Services, urgent jobs, specialist skills | Needs a real difference |
| **Psychological** | ₹99, ₹199, price points, anchors | Low and mid-ticket retail | Mixed evidence |
| **Bundle** | Several items or services for one price | Raising bill size, moving slow stock | GST on mixed bundles; giving away margin on items that sell anyway |

Most shops blend them: cost-plus for the floor, competitors to set the price, value to decide where to charge more, and a ₹99 ending or combo to finish.

## Step 1: Know your real cost

Most pricing mistakes start with a wrong cost. Your **landing cost** per unit is:

**Landing cost = Purchase price + freight + packing + wastage share (+ purchase GST, if you cannot claim it back)**

The GST line depends on your registration:

| Your GST status | GST paid on purchases | What counts as cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regular GST-registered | Claimed back as input tax credit | Price **before** GST |
| Composition dealer | Cannot be claimed | Price **including** GST |
| Not registered | Cannot be claimed | Price **including** GST |

For a **service**, the cost is mostly time. Count your own hours at a fair rate, plus travel, consumables and a share of rent, phone and tools, or you will end up working for free.

## Step 2: Cost-plus pricing, and the markup vs margin trap

Cost-plus is the simplest method: decide a markup and add it to cost. Its weakness is that people confuse **markup** (profit as a share of cost) with **margin** (profit as a share of the selling price), and set prices that earn less than they think.

- **Markup = (Price − Cost) ÷ Cost × 100**
- **Margin = (Price − Cost) ÷ Price × 100**
- **Price for a target margin = Cost ÷ (1 − margin)**

| If you mark up by | Your margin is | To get this margin | Mark up by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10% | 9.09% | 10% | 11.11% |
| 20% | 16.67% | 15% | 17.65% |
| 25% | 20% | 20% | 25% |
| 33.33% | 25% | 25% | 33.33% |
| 50% | 33.33% | 30% | 42.86% |
| 100% | 50% | 40% | 66.67% |

**Example:** an item costs ₹80 and you want a 20% margin. Adding 20% gives ₹96, which is a margin of only ₹16 ÷ ₹96 = 16.67%. The right price is ₹80 ÷ 0.80 = **₹100**. More in our [profit margin formula](https://billzap.app/blog/profit-margin-formula) guide.

Cost-plus is the right **starting point**, but it says nothing about what the customer will pay.

## Step 3: Competitor-based pricing

Customers know the price of what they buy every week (milk, atta, oil, branded biscuits). Being ₹5 higher than the shop down the lane is noticed. How to use competitors without a price war:

1. **List your 20 to 30 "known value" items** and check two nearby shops and one delivery app once a month.
2. **Stay close on those items**, even at a thin margin. They decide whether your shop "feels expensive".
3. **Earn your margin elsewhere**: loose spices, dry fruits, personal care and own-packed items that customers do not price-check.
4. **Do not chase a supermarket's or app's lowest price.** Compete on speed, credit for trusted customers, delivery and opening hours.

## Step 4: Value-based pricing

Value-based pricing asks: **what is this worth to the customer, compared with their next best option?** A tailor who finishes a blouse in two days before a wedding, or an electrician who arrives within the hour, can charge more than one who needs a week. That is not overcharging, as long as you quote clearly before the work.

## Step 5: Psychological pricing (₹99 and friends)

Indian retail is full of ₹99, ₹199 and ₹999 prices, on the idea that ₹99 feels nearer ₹90 than ₹100. The evidence is weaker than the habit: a study from IIM Bangalore on how Indian consumers process price endings found that only about 30% of respondents who recalled a price read it left to right, though shoppers do link a 9 ending with value. A large online experiment published in 2022 also failed to reproduce the left-digit effect.

Use 9-endings on mid-ticket items (₹149, ₹299); for a ₹20 chai, round numbers save change. Use price points customers know (₹5, ₹10, ₹20 packs), put the big pack beside the small one so the per-kg saving shows, and test: try ₹119 instead of ₹115 on one item for a month and compare units sold.

## Step 6: Bundle pricing

A bundle sells several items together for one price: a pooja kit, a festive hamper, "AC service plus gas check". It raises bill size and clears slow stock paired with a fast seller.

**GST catch:** if items with **different GST rates** are sold together for **a single price**, GST law treats it as a **mixed supply** and taxes the whole bundle at the **highest rate** among the items (Section 8 of the CGST Act). A hamper of 5% food items plus an 18% gift box at one price is taxed at 18% on the full amount. To avoid that, show each item with its own price on the bill and offer the "combo discount" as a line discount.

## MRP rules: what the law allows

- **MRP is the maximum, inclusive of all taxes.** The Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011 define the retail sale price as the maximum price at which a packaged commodity may be sold to the consumer, inclusive of all taxes.
- **No one may sell above MRP.** Rule 18(2) bars retailers, wholesalers, manufacturers, packers and importers alike.
- **You cannot add GST on top.** Showing GST separately on your bill does not allow charging above MRP; the GST comes out of the MRP.
- **Selling below MRP is allowed.** A "cooling charge" that takes a packaged item above MRP is not.
- **When GST rates change**, companies may sticker a revised MRP. After the 22 September 2025 rate cuts, revised MRPs on old stock were allowed until 31 March 2026, with the original MRP left visible. Follow the revised price your supplier communicates.
- **Loose goods** weighed in front of the customer carry no MRP, but your scale must be verified.

Customers can complain to the state Legal Metrology department or the National Consumer Helpline (**1915**). Penalties under the Legal Metrology Act, 2009 have been revised recently, so check your state department's current rules.

## GST-inclusive pricing maths

A **regular GST-registered** shop keeps only the taxable value of an inclusive price:

- **Taxable value = Inclusive price × 100 ÷ (100 + GST rate)**
- **GST = Inclusive price − Taxable value**

So at 5%, GST is 4.76% of an inclusive price; at 18%, it is 15.25%. Our guide on [how to calculate GST](https://billzap.app/blog/how-to-calculate-gst) has the full method and current rates.

### Worked example 1: a packaged kirana item

A regular GST-registered kirana buys a 400 g pack of branded namkeen. In this example it is taxed at 5% (always check the item's HSN rate). The distributor bills **₹95 + 5% GST = ₹99.75**. The pack's MRP is **₹120**. The shop claims the ₹4.75 back as credit, so its cost is **₹95**.

Three possible shelf prices:

| Shelf price (incl. GST) | Taxable value | GST | Profit | Margin | Markup |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₹120 (MRP) | ₹114.29 | ₹5.71 | ₹19.29 | 16.88% | 20.30% |
| ₹119 | ₹113.33 | ₹5.67 | ₹18.33 | 16.18% | 19.30% |
| ₹115 (matching a rival) | ₹109.52 | ₹5.48 | ₹14.52 | 13.26% | 15.28% |

First line: ₹120 × 100 ÷ 105 = ₹114.29; profit ₹114.29 − ₹95 = ₹19.29; margin ₹19.29 ÷ ₹114.29 = 16.88%.

Dropping from ₹120 to ₹115 is a ₹5 cut to the customer but about a **25% cut in profit** per pack (₹19.29 to ₹14.52). Worth it only if it brings in customers who buy higher-margin items too.

In a **composition** shop, the cost is ₹99.75 (no credit) and composition tax for a trader is 1% of turnover (₹1.20), so profit at MRP is ₹120 − ₹99.75 − ₹1.20 = **₹19.05**. See the [GST composition scheme](https://billzap.app/blog/gst-composition-scheme) guide.

### Worked example 2: pricing a service

A GST-registered AC repair business is pricing a standard AC service visit, taxed at 18%.

| Cost per visit | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Technician time, 1.5 hours at ₹200 | ₹300 |
| Travel | ₹80 |
| Cleaning chemicals and consumables | ₹60 |
| Share of overheads (phone, tools, rent, bike) | ₹100 |
| **Full cost** | **₹540** |

**Cost-plus for a 40% margin:** ₹540 ÷ 0.60 = **₹900** before GST. With 18% GST the customer pays ₹900 + ₹162 = **₹1,062**.

**Competitor check:** suppose local technicians quote ₹700 to ₹900 and an app shows ₹999 including tax. A bill of ₹1,062 looks expensive next to ₹999.

**Value and psychology:** quote **₹999 including GST**, with a free revisit within 30 days if cooling drops.

| Quote | Taxable value | GST | Profit | Margin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₹900 + GST (₹1,062) | ₹900.00 | ₹162.00 | ₹360.00 | 40.00% |
| ₹999 including GST | ₹846.61 | ₹152.39 | ₹306.61 | 36.22% |

The ₹999 quote gives up ₹53.39 per visit but matches the price customers have seen. **Always say whether your quote includes GST**: "₹900" versus "₹900 + GST" is a ₹162 argument at the door. A provider below the [GST registration limit](https://billzap.app/blog/gst-registration-limit) charges no GST at all, a real advantage on consumer jobs.

## When discounting hurts

A discount comes straight out of your margin. The extra sales you need to earn the same profit are:

**Extra units needed = Discount ÷ (Margin − Discount)**

| Your margin | 5% discount needs | 10% discount needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10% | 100% more units | Impossible: zero profit |
| 15% | 50% more | 200% more |
| 20% | 33% more | 100% more |
| 30% | 20% more | 50% more |
| 40% | 14% more | 33% more |

Discounts hurt most when **the margin is thin** (a "10% off" sale on 5% to 10% staples is a loss), when **the item sells anyway**, when **it becomes a habit** and customers wait for the sale, and when it is **bargaining at the counter**: ₹10 off each of 500 bills a month is ₹5,000.

Better options: **quantity pricing** ("2 for ₹110"), **bundles** with a slow mover, or a **free delivery threshold**. If you must discount, pick high-margin items, set an end date, and check the [break-even point](https://billzap.app/blog/break-even-point) first.

## How to price a product: a six-step checklist

1. **Work out the full landing cost** (or cost per job).
2. **Set a floor**: Cost ÷ (1 − target margin).
3. **Check two competitors and one app.**
4. **Decide your position**: match on known-value items, charge more where you add speed or trust.
5. **Check the ceiling**: MRP, or the customer's next best option.
6. **Finish the price** with an ending or a bundle, and review it monthly.

## FAQ

### What is the best pricing strategy for a small business?

There is no single best one. Use cost-plus for a floor, match competitors on items customers compare, and charge more where you offer speed, credit or expertise.

### How do I calculate the selling price from cost?

For a target margin, divide: Selling price = Cost ÷ (1 − margin). For a 25% margin on a ₹60 cost, that is ₹60 ÷ 0.75 = ₹80. Adding 25% to cost gives ₹75, which is only a 20% margin.

### Can a shopkeeper charge more than MRP?

No. Under the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011, no one may sell a packaged commodity above its MRP, and MRP already includes all taxes. Selling below MRP is allowed.

### Can I add GST on top of MRP?

No. MRP is inclusive of all taxes. A GST-registered shop must work out the GST from within the MRP: at 5%, taxable value is MRP × 100 ÷ 105.

### Should I quote prices with or without GST?

To consumers, quote the final price including GST. To business customers who claim input tax credit, "₹X + GST" is common. Either way, say which it is.

### Does ₹99 pricing really work?

Shoppers link 9-endings with value, but research on sales lift is mixed. Test it on a few mid-ticket items first.

### When should a small business avoid discounts?

When the margin is thin, the item already sells well, or discounts become routine. On a 20% margin, a 10% discount needs twice the sales for the same profit.

## The bottom line

**Cost tells you the lowest price you can live with; customers and competitors tell you the highest you can get.** For packaged goods, MRP is a legal ceiling that includes GST. Get the cost right, use the margin formula rather than a rough markup, go easy on discounts for thin-margin items, and check margins monthly. Small price decisions, repeated on hundreds of bills, are where profit is made or lost.
