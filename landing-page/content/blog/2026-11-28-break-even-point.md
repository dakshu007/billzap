Every shop owner carries a rough version of this number in their head: "I need about ₹15,000 a day to cover everything." The **break even point** is that number done properly. It is the level of sales at which your shop makes neither a profit nor a loss. Below it, every day costs you money. Above it, every extra sale adds profit.

Most pages on this topic use a factory making widgets. A shop does not sell one product, and a tea stall counts cups, not units of output. This guide gives the formula both ways, works it through for a **tea shop** and a **kirana**, and covers what textbooks leave out: GST inside your prices, your own salary, loan EMIs and the sales mix.

## Quick answer

- **Break even point in units** = Fixed costs ÷ (Selling price per unit − Variable cost per unit)
- **Break even point in rupees** = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin ratio
- **Contribution margin** = Selling price − Variable cost. **Contribution margin ratio** = Contribution ÷ Selling price.
- **Margin of safety** = (Actual sales − Break-even sales) ÷ Actual sales × 100
- **Tea shop example:** ₹44,000 fixed costs a month, ₹15 a cup, ₹6.80 variable cost a cup: break-even is **5,366 cups a month, about 179 cups a day**.
- **Kirana example:** ₹65,000 fixed costs, 13.5% contribution ratio: break-even is **₹4,81,481 of sales a month, about ₹16,050 a day**.
- Use prices **before GST**, and include **your own salary** in fixed costs, or the answer is too low.

## Fixed costs and variable costs in a shop

The whole calculation rests on splitting your costs correctly.

- **Fixed costs** stay the same whether you sell a lot or a little in a month.
- **Variable costs** rise and fall with each sale.

| Cost | Fixed or variable | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shop rent | Fixed | |
| Salaries of helpers | Fixed | Variable only if paid per piece or per day worked |
| **Your own salary** | Fixed | The one most owners leave out |
| Electricity | Mostly fixed | A fridge or freezer runs regardless of sales |
| Internet, phone, software | Fixed | |
| Loan interest | Fixed | See the EMI note below |
| Annual costs (licences, repairs, festival bonus, insurance) | Fixed | Divide by 12 and add to each month |
| Cost of goods sold | Variable | The biggest variable cost in any shop |
| Packing, carry bags, paper cups | Variable | |
| Wastage, expiry, breakage | Variable | Grows with volume |
| Composition GST (1% for traders) | Variable | Charged on turnover |
| Delivery cost per order | Variable | |

Mixed costs, such as electricity that rises a little with longer hours, can be treated as fixed for a small shop. Precision matters less than being honest.

## The break even point formula

### In units

**Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit**

where **contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit**.

Each unit you sell "contributes" that much towards paying your fixed costs. Once enough units have covered the fixed costs, every further unit's contribution is profit. This works when you sell one main product, or products of similar price and cost: cups of tea, plates of momos, haircuts, photocopies.

### In rupees (sales value)

**Break-even sales = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin ratio**

where **contribution margin ratio = contribution ÷ selling price** (also called the **P/V ratio**).

This is the version a kirana, medical store or garment shop needs, because you sell hundreds of items at different prices. Your contribution ratio is roughly your **average gross margin**, less any other variable cost such as packing or wastage. If you are unsure how to work out margin, see our guide to the [profit margin formula](https://billzap.app/blog/profit-margin-formula).

### Margin of safety

**Margin of safety = (Actual sales − Break-even sales) ÷ Actual sales × 100**

It tells you how far sales can fall before you start losing money. A margin of safety of 20% means a bad month with 20% lower sales takes you to zero profit, not into a loss. Below 10% is fragile.

### Sales needed for a target profit

**Required sales = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ Contribution margin ratio**

Use the units version for a single product: (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ Contribution per unit.

## Worked example 1: a tea shop

Ravi runs a tea stall near a bus depot. He sells tea at **₹15 a cup**.

**Variable cost per cup:**

| Item | Cost per cup |
| --- | --- |
| Milk | ₹4.00 |
| Tea leaves | ₹0.80 |
| Sugar | ₹0.70 |
| Gas | ₹0.50 |
| Paper cup | ₹0.80 |
| **Total** | **₹6.80** |

Contribution per cup = ₹15 − ₹6.80 = **₹8.20**. Contribution ratio = ₹8.20 ÷ ₹15 = **54.7%**.

**Fixed costs per month:**

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | ₹12,000 |
| Helper | ₹10,000 |
| Electricity | ₹1,000 |
| Licences, repairs, sundries | ₹1,000 |
| Ravi's own salary | ₹20,000 |
| **Total** | **₹44,000** |

**Break-even in cups** = ₹44,000 ÷ ₹8.20 = 5,365.9, so **5,366 cups a month**. Over 30 days, about **179 cups a day**.

**Break-even in rupees** = ₹44,000 ÷ (₹8.20 ÷ ₹15) = **₹80,488 a month** (5,366 cups × ₹15 = ₹80,490; the ₹2 difference is rounding).

**Margin of safety.** Ravi actually sells about 220 cups a day, or 6,600 a month: sales of ₹99,000. Margin of safety = (₹99,000 − ₹80,488) ÷ ₹99,000 = **18.7%**. His profit over and above his own ₹20,000 salary is 6,600 × ₹8.20 − ₹44,000 = **₹10,120**.

**What if?**

| Change | Contribution per cup | Break-even cups a day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base case | ₹8.20 | 179 |
| Price raised to ₹16 | ₹9.20 | 160 |
| Milk costs ₹1 more per cup | ₹7.20 | 204 |
| Ravi leaves out his own salary | ₹8.20 | 98 |

The last row is the trap. Without his salary, Ravi "breaks even" at 98 cups and thinks every cup after that is profit. In fact he is only paying himself. A one-rupee rise in milk cost pushes his break-even up by 25 cups a day, which is why tea sellers watch milk prices so closely. ## Worked example 2: a kirana store

Sunita's kirana sells about **₹6,00,000 a month**. Her blended gross margin across all items is **14%**, and carry bags, packing and expiry cost about 0.5% of sales. So her **contribution ratio is 13.5%**.

**Fixed costs per month:**

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | ₹20,000 |
| Helper | ₹12,000 |
| Electricity (two fridges, lights) | ₹3,000 |
| Phone, internet, sundries | ₹2,000 |
| Interest on a shop loan | ₹3,000 |
| Sunita's own salary | ₹25,000 |
| **Total** | **₹65,000** |

**Break-even sales** = ₹65,000 ÷ 0.135 = **₹4,81,481 a month**, about **₹16,050 a day** over 30 days.

**Margin of safety** = (₹6,00,000 − ₹4,81,481) ÷ ₹6,00,000 = **19.8%**.

**Profit** = ₹6,00,000 × 13.5% − ₹65,000 = **₹16,000** a month, after paying herself ₹25,000.

### Break-even table for a shop

Find your fixed costs (rows) and your contribution ratio (columns) to read your monthly break-even sales.

| Monthly fixed costs | 10% contribution | 13.5% contribution | 18% contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ₹40,000 | ₹4,00,000 | ₹2,96,296 | ₹2,22,222 |
| ₹65,000 | ₹6,50,000 | ₹4,81,481 | ₹3,61,111 |
| ₹90,000 | ₹9,00,000 | ₹6,66,667 | ₹5,00,000 |

Divide by 30 for a daily target. Low-margin shops (grocery staples, mobile phones) need far more sales to break even than high-margin ones (cosmetics, gifts, food service) with the same rent.

### Why the sales mix matters

Sunita's 13.5% is an average. If customers shift towards atta, oil and sugar, which carry thin margins, and away from snacks and personal care, her average drops and her break-even rises, even though total sales look the same. Check your margin by category every few months, not just total sales.

## Five ways to lower your break even point

1. **Raise your contribution ratio.** Buy better (direct from distributors, schemes, bulk on fast movers), stock more high-margin lines and cut discounting. For Sunita, moving from 13.5% to 15% lowers break-even from ₹4,81,481 to **₹4,33,333**, a drop of ₹48,148 a month.
2. **Cut fixed costs.** Renegotiate rent, switch to LED lighting, drop a subscription you do not use. Cutting ₹5,000 of fixed costs lowers Sunita's break-even to **₹4,44,444**, a drop of ₹37,037.
3. **Reduce wastage and expiry.** Every rupee of wastage is a variable cost that lowers contribution. First-in-first-out shelving and smaller, more frequent orders help.
4. **Price deliberately.** A small rise on items where customers do not compare prices can move break-even more than a big push for volume. Our guide to [pricing strategy](https://billzap.app/blog/pricing-strategy) covers how to do it without losing customers.
5. **Add sales that use existing fixed costs.** Home delivery, a phone-order list or a morning milk and bread counter add contribution without new rent.

In Sunita's numbers, 1.5 points of margin is worth more than ₹5,000 of cost cutting. For most shops, **margin is the bigger lever**, which is why buying well matters so much.

## Common mistakes that make break-even wrong

- **Counting GST as sales.** If you are a regular GST-registered shop, the GST you collect belongs to the government. Work with prices **before GST**. A tea shop registered under GST at 5% that charges ₹15 including tax actually earns ₹14.29 a cup; a kirana whose ₹6 lakh of sales includes ₹30,000 of GST really sold ₹5.7 lakh.
- **Forgetting composition tax.** A composition dealer cannot collect GST from customers but pays 1% of turnover (traders). Treat it as a variable cost. See our guide to the [GST composition scheme](https://billzap.app/blog/gst-composition-scheme).
- **Leaving out the owner's salary.** As the tea shop showed, it can halve your apparent break-even. If you would earn ₹20,000 working elsewhere, that is a real cost of running the shop.
- **Using markup instead of margin.** A 25% markup is a 20% margin. Plug markup into the formula and your break-even comes out too low.
- **Using MRP instead of your actual selling price.** Discounts, free items and round-offs all cut contribution.
- **Ignoring annual and irregular costs.** Licence renewals, repairs, Diwali bonuses and insurance belong in monthly fixed costs, divided by 12.
- **Confusing profit break-even with cash break-even.** The principal part of a loan EMI is not an expense in your profit and loss, but it is cash leaving the shop. For a **cash break-even**, add the principal part of your EMI to fixed costs. A shop that is profitable on paper can still run short of cash; our guide to [working capital](https://billzap.app/blog/working-capital) explains why.

## FAQ

### What is the break even point?

It is the level of sales, in units or in rupees, at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither profit nor loss. Every sale above it adds profit.

### What is the formula for break even point?

In units: fixed costs ÷ (selling price per unit − variable cost per unit). In rupees: fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio, where the ratio is contribution divided by selling price.

### How do I calculate break even point for a shop with many products?

Use the rupee version. Take your average gross margin across all items, subtract other variable costs such as packing and wastage as a percentage of sales, and divide your monthly fixed costs by that ratio. Divide by 30 for a daily target.

### What is the difference between contribution margin and profit margin?

Contribution margin subtracts only variable costs from sales. Net profit margin also subtracts fixed costs such as rent and salaries. Contribution is what pays for the fixed costs; whatever remains is profit.

### What is a good margin of safety?

The higher the better. A margin of safety of 20% or more means sales can fall by a fifth before you make a loss. Below 10%, one bad month can push the shop into a loss.

### Should GST be included when calculating break even?

No, for a regular GST-registered shop. Use sales and costs before GST, because the GST you collect is paid to the government. Composition dealers should treat their composition tax as a variable cost.

### Should I include my own salary in fixed costs?

Yes. Without it, the break-even point only tells you when the shop stops losing money, not when it pays you for your time.

## The bottom line

The break even point turns a feeling ("I need about ₹15,000 a day") into a number you can check each evening. Split your costs into fixed and variable, include your own salary, work with prices before GST, and divide fixed costs by your contribution: per unit for a single product, by the contribution ratio for a shop. Then watch your margin of safety. If it is thin, a better buying margin usually helps more than cutting costs.
