A fruit and vegetable shop looks like the simplest business on the street: buy in the morning, sell by night. The catch is that your stock loses value every hour, and every kilo you throw away comes straight out of your profit. The shops that last buy the right quantity, price for the wastage they know is coming, and sell out before the stock turns.

This guide covers what other "how to start" articles skip: the arithmetic of wastage and shrinkage, pricing by weight, the rules on weighing scales, why most sabzi shops need no GST registration (and when that changes), and a worked daily profit and loss you can redo with your own numbers.

## Quick answer

- **Buy** at your nearest wholesale mandi early in the morning, daily for leafy greens and soft fruit, every two or three days for onion and potato.
- **Price for wastage:** effective cost = purchase cost ÷ (1 − expected loss). Then add your margin. Ignore wastage and a "25% margin" quietly becomes 17%.
- **Licences:** shop and establishment registration and a municipal trade licence (as your state requires), **FSSAI basic registration** (₹100 a year up to ₹1.5 crore turnover), and a **Legal Metrology stamped scale**. Cart vendors need a vending certificate from the town vending committee.
- **GST:** fresh fruits and vegetables are **exempt**. A shop selling **only** fresh produce does not need GST registration at any turnover. Add taxable items (dry fruits, frozen vegetables, packaged juice) and your whole turnover, produce included, counts towards the ₹40 lakh limit.
- **Bills:** an unregistered shop gives a simple **bill**; a registered shop selling exempt produce gives a **bill of supply**, not a tax invoice.
- **Profit** depends far more on wastage than on price. In our example, wastage rising from 8% to 15% wipes out 85% of the day's profit.

## Step 1: Choose your format and location

| Format | Suits | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cart (thela) or footpath stall** | Low capital, moving to where buyers are | Needs a vending certificate; no storage |
| **Small fixed shop** | Steady neighbourhood trade, phone orders | Rent eats margin if footfall is thin |
| **Shop plus home delivery** | Apartment clusters, office areas | Delivery cost and order mistakes |
| **Shop-in-kirana corner** | An existing grocery adding produce | Space and cooling for leafy items |

Location matters more than size: the walk home from a bus stop or station, apartment gates, or next to a milk booth that already pulls morning and evening crowds. Count footfall at 7 to 9 am and 6 to 9 pm on two weekdays and a Sunday before you sign a lease.

## Step 2: Learn to buy at the mandi

Most small shops buy from **commission agents (arhtiyas) or wholesalers** in the local APMC mandi or wholesale sabzi market. Commission rates and market fees **vary by state and by market**, and some states have taken fruits and vegetables out of APMC control. Ask two or three agents what they charge before you settle on one.

Habits that save money:

- **Go early.** The best lots go first, and prices often ease towards the end of the morning for what is left, but quality falls too.
- **Weigh the crate, not just the produce.** Note the tare (empty crate or bag weight) and check a few crates on your own scale.
- **Buy by turnover, not by price.** A cheap extra crate of tomatoes is not cheap if half of it rots on Thursday.
- **Write down every purchase**: item, weight, rate, commission and transport. You cannot price for wastage or know your margin without it.

Separate your stock into **fast perishables** (leafy greens, coriander, tomatoes, bananas, papaya, berries) bought daily in small lots, and **hardy stock** (onion, potato, garlic, ginger, pumpkin, apples, oranges) bought every two or three days.

## Step 3: Wastage and shrinkage, the numbers that decide your profit

Two kinds of loss eat into a sabzi shop:

- **Wastage:** produce that rots, bruises, gets crushed or is too ugly to sell.
- **Shrinkage:** produce that is still saleable but **weighs less** than when you bought it, because it loses water. Leafy greens and coriander lose weight fastest. Shrinkage also includes the coriander and green chillies you give away "free" with purchases, and the odd short weight.

Losses start long before your shop. A NABCONS study for the Ministry of Food Processing Industries (reference years 2020-22) estimated harvest and post-harvest losses at **6.02% to 15.05% for fruits** and **4.87% to 11.61% for vegetables**, up to the market. Your shop's own losses come on top of that, and only your records can tell you what they are. Weigh what you throw away every night for a month, item by item.

### The pricing formula that builds in wastage

**Effective cost per kg = purchase cost per kg ÷ (1 − expected loss)**

**Selling price = effective cost ÷ (1 − target margin)**

Take tomatoes bought at a landed cost of ₹32/kg (mandi rate plus your share of commission and transport), with 10% expected loss.

- Effective cost = ₹32 ÷ 0.90 = **₹35.56/kg**
- For a 25% margin: ₹35.56 ÷ 0.75 = ₹47.41, round to **₹48/kg**

Check it: buy 10 kg for ₹320, lose 1 kg, sell 9 kg at ₹48 = ₹432. Profit ₹112, which is **25.9%** of sales. Now price the same tomatoes ignoring wastage, at ₹32 ÷ 0.75 = ₹42.67, rounded to ₹43: 9 kg × ₹43 = ₹387, profit ₹67, a margin of just **17.3%**.

| Item (illustrative) | Landed cost/kg | Expected loss | Effective cost | Price for 25% margin | Rounded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Onion | ₹28 | 4% | ₹29.17 | ₹38.89 | ₹40 |
| Tomato | ₹32 | 10% | ₹35.56 | ₹47.41 | ₹48 |
| Palak (by weight) | ₹24 | 20% | ₹30.00 | ₹40.00 | ₹40 |
| Apple | ₹110 | 8% | ₹119.57 | ₹159.42 | ₹160 |

These prices are examples, not market rates. **The higher an item's loss, the bigger the gap between what it cost you and what you must charge.** For margin versus markup and rounding, see our guide to the [profit margin formula](https://billzap.app/blog/profit-margin-formula).

## Step 4: Pricing by weight at the counter

- **Show the price per kg** on a board for every item, and update it each morning. Customers trust a shop that shows rates.
- **Work out the 250 g and 500 g prices in advance** for your top items, so the counter moves quickly. At ₹48/kg, 250 g is ₹12 and 500 g is ₹24.
- **Round in whole rupees** that make change easy, and accept UPI for everything. A [UPI QR code for your shop](https://billzap.app/blog/upi-qr-code-for-shop) solves most change problems.
- **Price by piece or bunch** where weight makes no sense: coconuts, bunches of coriander, a dozen bananas. Be consistent.
- **Mark down early, not late.** Ripe bananas sold at a small discount at 5 pm are better than black bananas thrown out at 10 pm.

## Step 5: The weighing scale and Legal Metrology

Every scale used for trade must be **verified and stamped** by your state's **Legal Metrology** (weights and measures) department under the Legal Metrology Act, 2009, and **re-verified at the intervals your state's rules set**. Using an unstamped or faulty scale can lead to fines.

What to do:

- Buy an **electronic scale of an approved model** from a dealer who can arrange the first verification, and keep the certificate at the shop.
- Note the **re-verification due date** on the certificate and diarise it.
- Place the scale so the **customer can see the display**, tare out the bag or tray, and never round the weight in your favour.
- Weigh in **kilograms and grams**, not traditional units.

## Step 6: Licences and registrations

| Registration | Needed? | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shop and establishment registration** | For a fixed shop, in most states | State labour department; see our [shop act registration](https://billzap.app/blog/shop-act-registration) guide |
| **Trade licence** | Where your municipality requires one | Municipal corporation or council |
| **FSSAI basic registration** | Yes, as a food business | foscos.fssai.gov.in, ₹100 a year up to ₹1.5 crore turnover; see our [FSSAI registration](https://billzap.app/blog/fssai-registration) guide |
| **Legal Metrology verification** | Yes, for every scale | State Legal Metrology office |
| **Street vending certificate** | For carts and stalls | Town vending committee under the Street Vendors Act, 2014 |
| **GST registration** | Not if you sell only fresh produce | See the next section |
| **Udyam** | Optional | Helps with priority sector loans |

**On ripening:** never use calcium carbide ("masala") to ripen mangoes, bananas or papaya. It is prohibited under Regulation 2.3.5 of the FSS (Prohibition and Restrictions on Sales) Regulations, 2011, and FSSAI repeated the warning in an advisory dated 16 April 2026. Ethylene gas in proper ripening chambers, at up to 100 ppm, is permitted; buy from wholesalers who use it.

**Loans for carts and stalls:** the **PM SVANidhi** scheme, extended to 31 March 2030, offers street vendors working-capital loans of **₹15,000, then ₹25,000, then ₹50,000** as each is repaid. Ask at your urban local body or bank.

## Step 7: GST, and what kind of bill to give

**Fresh fruits and vegetables carry no GST.** They are exempt, and the September 2025 rate changes did not alter that.

**Do you need GST registration?** Under Section 23 of the CGST Act, a person who supplies **only** exempt goods does not have to register, whatever the turnover. So a shop selling nothing but fresh produce needs no GSTIN, even at ₹1 crore a year.

That changes the moment you sell **taxable** items too. Dry fruits and nuts, frozen vegetables, packaged juices, pickles and similar products carry GST (dry fruits and nuts moved to 5% from 22 September 2025). Once you sell any of them, your **aggregate turnover includes your exempt produce sales**, and you must register when the total crosses **₹40 lakh** (goods only, most states; ₹20 lakh in Manipur, Mizoram, Nagaland and Tripura). Our guide to the [GST registration limit](https://billzap.app/blog/gst-registration-limit) has the full rules.

**Which bill to give:**

| Your shop | Bill heading |
| --- | --- |
| Not registered under GST | **Bill** or cash memo, no GST shown |
| Registered, selling exempt produce | **Bill of supply**, no GST charged |
| Registered, selling a taxable item | **Tax invoice** with GST |

A registered shop selling both to a walk-in customer can issue a single invoice-cum-bill of supply under Rule 46A. Our guide to the [bill of supply](https://billzap.app/blog/bill-of-supply) explains the format.

## Step 8: Cold storage for a small shop

You do not need a cold room on day one. You need to slow down the stock that loses money fastest.

| Option | Good for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shade, airflow, crates off the floor** | Everything | Free. Keep produce out of direct sun and away from the road |
| **Wet gunny or cloth cover, water sprinkling** | Leafy greens, coriander | Cuts weight loss; do not soak, or rot speeds up |
| **Zero energy cool chamber** | Small shops without reliable power | A double brick wall with wet sand between, developed at IARI, New Delhi; keeps produce cooler and humid |
| **Visi cooler or display fridge** | Cut fruit, berries, mushrooms, greens | Electricity cost; worth it only for high-value items |
| **Small or solar cold room** | Larger shops and wholesalers | Ask your state horticulture department about subsidies before buying |

Store ethylene producers (ripe bananas, apples, tomatoes) away from greens and cucumbers, which yellow faster near them. Sell old stock first: put yesterday's produce in front.

## Step 9: A worked daily profit and loss

Here is an illustrative day for a small fixed shop. Replace every number with your own.

| | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Produce bought at mandi (incl. commission and fees) | ₹10,000 |
| Transport | ₹400 |
| **Cost of the day's stock** | **₹10,400** |
| Sold at full price: stock costing ₹8,500, at an average 40% markup on cost | ₹11,900 |
| Cleared at 7 pm at cost: stock costing ₹700 | ₹700 |
| Thrown away: stock costing ₹800 (8% wastage) | ₹0 |
| **Sales** | **₹12,600** |
| **Gross profit** (₹12,600 − ₹10,400) | **₹2,200** |
| Rent ₹15,000/month | ₹500 |
| Helper ₹12,000/month | ₹400 |
| Electricity ₹1,500/month | ₹50 |
| Bags, water, sundries | ₹100 |
| **Net profit for the day** | **₹1,150** |

That is about **₹34,500 over a 30-day month**, before paying yourself anything.

Now change one thing at a time:

| Scenario | Sales | Gross profit | Net profit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base: 8% wastage, 40% markup | ₹12,600 | ₹2,200 | ₹1,150 |
| Wastage rises to 15% (full-price sales fall to stock costing ₹7,800) | ₹11,620 | ₹1,220 | ₹170 |
| Markup cut to 30%, wastage 8% | ₹11,750 | ₹1,350 | ₹300 |

Seven extra kilos in every hundred going into the bin cost more than a 10-point price cut. That is why the best sabzi sellers obsess over buying quantity and stock rotation. ## FAQ

### Is GST registration required for a vegetable shop?

Not if you sell only fresh fruits and vegetables, which are exempt. A person supplying only exempt goods need not register at any turnover. If you also sell taxable items, register once your total turnover, including produce, crosses ₹40 lakh (₹20 lakh in some north-eastern states).

### Is FSSAI registration needed for a fruit and vegetable shop?

Yes. Fruit and vegetable sellers are food businesses. Small shops need basic registration on foscos.fssai.gov.in, at ₹100 a year for turnover up to ₹1.5 crore.

### How much wastage is normal in a vegetable shop?

It depends on what you sell, the season and how well you buy and store. Leafy greens lose far more than onions. Weigh what you discard every night for a month to find your own figure, then price each item for it.

### How do I set selling prices for vegetables?

Divide your landed cost per kg by (1 − expected loss) to get the effective cost, then divide by (1 − target margin). Tomatoes at ₹32/kg with 10% loss and a 25% margin come to ₹48/kg.

### Does my weighing scale need a stamp?

Yes. Any scale used for trade must be verified and stamped by your state's Legal Metrology department and re-verified at the intervals your state's rules set. Keep the certificate at the shop.

### Which bill should a fruit shop give?

An unregistered shop gives a plain bill or cash memo. A GST-registered shop gives a bill of supply for exempt produce and a tax invoice for taxable items.

## The bottom line

A fruit and vegetable shop is a daily game of buying the right amount, pricing for the loss you know is coming, and selling before the stock turns. Register with FSSAI, get your scale stamped, and skip GST registration if you sell only fresh produce, but check again the day you add dry fruits or packaged items. Track your purchases, your wastage and your daily sales from the first week, and the numbers will tell you what to change.

*This guide is general information, not legal or tax advice. Licence rules and fees vary by state and city; check with your local authority before you open.*
